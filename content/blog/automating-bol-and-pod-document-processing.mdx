Small freight brokerages and 3PLs post a particular job ad. The title varies,
accounts payable admin, billing coordinator, operations assistant, but the
duties are recognisable: receive documents from carriers, check them against
the load, key the details into the system, chase what is missing, file it.

That ad is a description of a workflow, and about half of it is automatable
with current tools. The other half is not, and the failure mode in this
category is a vendor who cannot tell you which half is which. So here is the
split.

Two things to state up front. I have scoped this workflow and built the
document-handling patterns it depends on, but I have not yet delivered one of
these in a 3PL. Where I am reasoning from the general shape of document
intake rather than from a specific delivered system, I say so.

## What the documents are

**The bill of lading (BOL)** is the document that travels with the freight. It
records what is being shipped, who is shipping it, who is receiving it, and
under what terms. It is the contract of carriage.

**The proof of delivery (POD)** is the signed confirmation that the freight
arrived. It is the document that makes the load billable, which is why it is
the one everyone chases.

The operational reality is that both arrive as email attachments, mostly PDFs,
frequently scans, sometimes photographs of paper taken in a truck cab. Each
carrier's format is different. Nothing is standardised, and this is the entire
difficulty.

## The half that automates well

**Classification.** Deciding whether an inbound attachment is a BOL, a POD, an
invoice, a rate confirmation, or something irrelevant. Models are reliable at
this even on poor scans, because it is a judgement about document type rather
than an exact reading.

**Field extraction.** Pulling the load reference, dates, carrier, addresses,
piece count, weight from a layout the system has never seen before. This is
where the current generation genuinely changed things. Template-based
extraction required a template per carrier and broke when anyone changed their
form. A model reads an unfamiliar layout without one, which is what makes the
long tail of small carriers tractable.

**Matching to the load.** Taking the extracted reference and finding the
corresponding record. Mostly ordinary lookup logic, with fuzzy matching for
the reference numbers people mistype.

**Chasing what is missing.** Noticing that a load delivered four days ago has
no POD attached, and sending the carrier a reminder naming the load. Not
sophisticated, and it recovers a surprising amount of time, because it is the
task humans deprioritise when busy.

**Exception triage.** Sorting the problems into "someone must look at this"
versus "this is fine," so the human queue is short and genuinely needs a
human.

## The half that must stay deterministic

This is the part that matters, and where I would be sceptical of anyone
promising full automation.

**Anything that becomes money.** The extracted values must not flow
unreviewed into an invoice. Not because extraction is bad, but because the
error mode is silent: a plausible wrong weight or piece count produces a
plausible wrong invoice, and you find out from a customer dispute weeks later.
Extraction proposes, a rule validates, and a human approves anything outside
tolerance.

**Validation rules.** Does the piece count on the POD match the BOL. Is the
delivery date after the pickup date. Is the weight within a sane band for the
equipment type. These are ordinary business rules and they should be code, not
prompts. Deterministic checks are cheap, testable and explainable, and they
catch the exact category of error that fluent extraction produces.

**The confidence boundary.** Every extracted field needs a confidence, and
below a threshold it goes to a person. A system without an explicit "I am not
sure" path will confidently key a bad value, which is worse than not
automating at all, because it removes the human who would have noticed.

**Anything with a compliance dimension.** Hazmat, temperature-controlled
chains, customs. Adjacent to legal exposure, and not where to be clever.

## Getting it back into the TMS

This is where the project is won or lost, and where most estimates go wrong.

If the TMS has a decent API, the write-back is routine. Many of the systems in
this segment do not, or expose a subset that excludes exactly the fields you
need to update. When that happens you are looking at a database integration,
a file-based interface on a schedule, or driving the interface directly.

That variance is why I will not quote this workflow without knowing the
specific TMS. The document handling is fairly predictable. The write-back
ranges from an afternoon to the majority of the build, and the honest answer
to "how long does this take" is that the answer depends on a question I have
to ask first.

## How I would scope a first version

Not the whole workflow. One document type, one direction.

POD intake specifically: classify inbound attachments, extract the load
reference and delivery details, match to the load, flag anything below the
confidence threshold for review, and mark the load ready to bill. Leave BOL
handling, invoice generation and carrier chasing for a second pass.

The reason to start there is that POD is the document that gates billing, so
the value is immediate and measurable in days-to-invoice rather than in a
vague efficiency claim. It is also the narrowest slice that produces a real
outcome, which makes it a genuine two to three week
[fixed-price pilot](/blog/what-a-3000-dollar-ai-pilot-actually-buys) rather
than a phased programme.
