Most quotes you will get for "an AI tool for our business" are a range with no
scope attached. Somewhere between $15,000 and "let's start with a discovery
phase." That is not a price, it is a way of not answering the question until
you are already invested.

So here is the direct answer. A first engagement with me is a fixed price
between $3,000 and $6,000, it runs two to three weeks, and it produces one
narrowly scoped system that is running in production at the end of it. Not a
prototype, not a deck, not a roadmap. Something your team opens on a Monday
and uses.

Below is what that money actually covers, what it does not, and why the
number is fixed rather than hourly.

## What is in the $3,000 to $6,000

**One workflow, end to end.** The scope is deliberately a single job: the
document that gets read and filed, the lead that gets scored, the report that
gets assembled every Friday. Not "an AI layer for operations." One workflow,
picked because it is the one your team complains about most, and taken all
the way from input to the place the output has to land.

**The integration into where the work already happens.** A tool nobody opens
is worth nothing, so this is not a standalone app with its own login unless
there is a reason for one. It writes into the CRM, posts into the Slack
channel, drops the file into the shared drive, updates the row in the sheet.
The integration is not a phase two.

**The unglamorous reliability work.** Retries, rate limits, a cap on model
spend, and a defined behaviour for every failure path. This is the part that
separates a demo from a system, and it is the part most often quietly left
out of a cheaper quote. If the model is down, or slow, or refuses, the tool
still has to do something sensible rather than show your operations manager a
stack trace.

**The repository, in your account, from day one.** Not at the end, not on
final payment. You own the code, it runs on your infrastructure, and if we
never speak again you keep a working system. There is no per-seat licence and
no platform subscription to me.

**A written handover.** What it does, what it costs to run per month, where
the API keys live, what breaks first as volume grows, and what the next
sensible thing to build is. Two pages, plain English, written for whoever
inherits it and not for me.

**Direct access to the person building it.** There is no account manager
layer, because there is no account manager. You are talking to the developer,
which is the main reason a two week timeline is possible at all.

## What is deliberately not in it

**Custom model training.** I build on frontier model APIs. If your problem
genuinely requires a fine-tuned or self-hosted model, a pilot is the wrong
shape of engagement and I will say so before you pay anything.

**A multi-tenant product.** A pilot builds a system for your company, not a
SaaS you could resell. Those are different builds with different costs, and
conflating them is how a $5,000 project becomes a $50,000 one.

**Unlimited revisions.** Two rounds of feedback are in scope. Past that, we
are designing a different system, and the honest move is to price it
separately rather than absorb it and resent it.

**Data cleanup.** If the CRM has four years of duplicates, that is real work
and it is not two weeks of it. I will tell you what shape the data needs to
be in, and that is usually work your team can do faster and cheaper than I
can.

**Ongoing operation.** A pilot ends. If you want someone watching it, changing
it, and building the next three things, that is a retainer, and it starts at
$4,000 a month. But nothing about the pilot obligates you to it, and that is
the point.

## Why it is fixed price and not hourly

Hourly billing punishes the person who is fast, which is a strange thing to
select for when you are buying software. It also makes every clarifying
question feel expensive to you, which is exactly the conversation that
determines whether the build is any good.

More practically: an hourly quote transfers all the estimation risk onto you.
If I misjudge the work, you pay for my misjudgement. Fixed price puts that
risk on me, which is where it belongs, because I am the one who is supposed to
know how long this takes. The trade is that the scope has to be genuinely
narrow, and I will hold that line during the project.

## Why there is a floor, and why it is not free

I do not do free discovery calls that produce a written proposal. That
sounds like a small thing but it is the single biggest filter in this
business. Free scoping work attracts people who are collecting quotes to
benchmark an internal hire, or who wanted a free consultation and framed it
as a project. Both are real, both are expensive, and neither ends in a build.

A small paid pilot filters for the buyer who has an actual problem and a
budget line, and it shortens the sales cycle from months to about a week. It
is also, frankly, the fairest version: you risk $3,000 to find out whether
working together produces anything, instead of risking $40,000 on a proposal
that reads well.

## How the number moves inside the range

The $3,000 end is one clearly defined input, one clearly defined output, and
a system you already have an API for. Scoring inbound leads against a rubric
and writing the result back to HubSpot sits here.

The $6,000 end is where there are multiple input formats, a system with no
usable API that has to be driven another way, or a compliance constraint that
changes how data is allowed to move. Document intake from email attachments in
four different vendor formats sits here.

If it does not fit in $6,000 and three weeks, I will tell you that before you
pay, and I will tell you what the smaller first version looks like. Splitting a
large build into a pilot plus a follow-on is almost always better than
compressing it, because the follow-on gets designed with three weeks of real
usage data behind it instead of guesses.

## The honest caveat

I am a solo studio. That means you get the person who wrote the code on every
call, and it also means I take a limited number of these at a time and I am
not the right choice if you need twelve people staffed by Monday. If what you
actually need is a team, say so early and I will tell you plainly that this
is not the right fit rather than stretch to take the work.
